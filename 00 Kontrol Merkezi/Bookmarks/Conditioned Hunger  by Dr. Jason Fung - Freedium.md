In the 1890s, famed Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov was studying dogs who naturally salivate when they saw food. Astutely, he noted that the dogs began to salivate before seeing the food, when they saw the lab technician who normally brought it. The dogs learned that seeing the technician meant food was coming, and the dogs salivated in anticipation. The neutral stimulus, the technician, could provoke a conditioned response.

Pavlov undertook further experiments to pair another neutral stimulus, a bell ringing, with food. Soon the ringing of the bell would trigger salivation in the dogs, because they expected food. This is known as a learned, or conditioned response. Pavlov's dogs help revolutionize our understanding of behaviors, habits and reflexes.## [Cephalic Hunger](https://drjasonfung.medium.com/cephalic-hunger-1a8dbe4ee83b)

Why does this matter? Because hunger is also a conditioned response. We have learned through countless repetition that we eat three times a day, based on the time, no matter how hunger we are, and our hunger rises according to match.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*_CwrnsCO__Rhu114uN8m4A.jpeg)

Consider how the [hunger hormone ghrelin changes over the day. In the average person, it spikes three times a day,](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15941923/) slightly before breakfast, lunch and dinner. This is not a coincidence. This is a conditioned response. We normally eat three times a day, and we have learned to associate the times of approximately 8:00 am, 12:00 noon and 7:00 pm with food. Like Pavlov's dogs, our hunger rises in anticipation of food arriving. This has an important implication — much of our 'hunger' is learned behavior.

Eating frequently teaches you to be hungry frequently.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*zo0nBx-65AwsvJwZvx-vDA.jpeg)

Suppose you normally eat 6 times per day — three meals and three snacks. Ghrelin, and therefore hunger will spike 6 times a day. That's a problem. In the 2000s and 2010s, people were convinced that eating frequent small meals helped weight loss. Instead of [three times a day, the average American was eating more than 5 times per day](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25088521/) (2.8 meals and 2.3 snacks per day). Fasting was heavily discouraged. Skipping a meal? Whoo, boy, those were fighting words. Eating frequently was supposed to stabilize blood glucose levels, increase satiety, prevent hunger and prevent overeating. But no scientific studies actually [supported this belief](https://www.dietaryguidelines.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/ScientificReport_of_the_2020DietaryGuidelinesAdvisoryCommittee_first-print.pdf), and logic argued against it.

Constant eating usually makes [people more, not less hungry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23404961/). That is literally the entire point of an appetizer — a small, tasty morsel to make us **eat more**. The ancient Greeks and Romans stimulated their guest's appetite with little bits of fish, seasoned vegetables, cheese and olives. The Italian Renaissance writer Platina recommended thin rolls of grilled veal. This appetizing effect of a small bit of food is no secret. Anybody who has thrown a dinner party over the last 200 years know this.

Eating 6 appetizer sized 'meals' to make us hungry and then using our willpower to stop eating before we are full *sounds* stupid, because it *is* stupid. 'Grazing' works for cows, or if you want to look like one. Besides, eating constantly is a nuisance. When are you supposed to get your work done? You are endlessly thinking about what you need to eat and when to eat it.

It is obviously unnecessary to snack because our body stores food energy (calories) as body fat for the exact reason of providing calories when needed. Body fat exists precisely so that we do NOT need to constantly eat. Eating constantly is heavily promoted by and is only good for the food industry. Because of the inconvenience of constant eating, snacks are often pre-packaged, ultra-processed foods, full of chemical preservatives.

Inertia, one of Newton's fundamental laws of physics, states that an object at rest or in motion will stay that way unless acted upon. But inertia applies to many things in life, and can either work for you or against you. When my sons were little, I couldn't ever get them *into* the bath. Once they were in, I couldn't ever get them *out of* the bath. But this is normal, so use inertia to your advantage. If you are always eating, you'll want to continue eating. That's bad. If you are not eating, keep not eating. That's good.

I've noticed many times that I wasn't hungry, but ate anyway, usually for a social reason. As I began eating, my appetite returned, and I finished the entire meal relatively normally. Before eating, I could have happily skipped the meal. Once I started eating, I ate everything. The first few bites whet my appetite. Eating when you are not hungry is not a winning strategy for weight loss. Yet people are continually scolded for having the temerity to skip a single meal or snack.

#### Tip -Don't Eat all the Time. Give your body a chance to use the calories you ate.

If you normally eat only once or twice a day, you will only get hungry twice a day. You aren't expecting to eat, and therefore you don't get hungry. That's a huge, huge advantage if you are trying to lose weight. The problem is controlling the hunger, not the calories and managing hunger that comes twice a day is a whole lot easier than managing hunger that comes 6 times per day.

I started skipping breakfast routinely in medical school and have continued this for several decades. I was not trying to lose weight. I was always tired in the mornings and much preferred to sleep the extra 15 minutes rather than eat. Now I almost never find myself hungry in the morning. This make is easy for me to not eat.

The 24-hour graph of ghrelin reveals a second important clue. If you skip a meal, ghrelin does NOT continually increase. After the initial wave, hunger recedes. Ghrelin shows a "spontaneous decrease after approximately 2 h without food consumption". Remember a time that you were busy and worked right through lunch. At about 1:00 you were hungry, but by 3:00 pm, you were no longer hungry and probably felt normal.

What happened? When you didn't eat, your body simply took the energy (calories) it needed from stored sources — blood sugar or body fat. Once you've 'fed' yourself on your body fat, why would you be hungry? If you have 175,000 calories stored as body fat (50 pounds), then why does it matter if you take 600 of those calories out because you didn't eat lunch? It doesn't.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*Oq5x-WFvzSxei52aCSEx8Q.jpeg)

When you don't eat, hunger goes down, and body fat is reduced. Perfect! Your body is carrying fat (stored calories) so that you have a source of food energy if you don't eat. So use the body fat for what it is designed for.

During fasting (any period you are not eating), people are intimidated because they expect their hunger to continually build until they can't stand it anymore. But that's not what happens. Hunger recedes when you ignore it. Hunger comes in a wave and will eventually pass. Ride the waves — it passes. Knowing that the hunger is temporary is vital to dealing with the hunger.

Ghrelin usually goes up when your stomach is empty, and down when it is filled. That's often the justification for constant eating. But there is yet another way to reduce ghrelin. Leave the stomach empty long enough, and ghrelin will eventually go down. This works until the next mealtime, when the stimulus of the clock strikes again.

Interestingly, when you fast for several days, ghrelin continues to drop. 24-hour ghrelin levels are about [25% lower than normal after 3 days of fasting](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15941923/). As your body continues to 'feed' itself with body fat, hunger diminishes. This is not some voodoo psychology trick. It's physiology. Ghrelin is lower. You are less hungry. The longer you fast, the easier it gets.

Many of my patients have experienced this phenomenon. They expect to be ravenously hungry after fasting but discover that their hunger has receded. They always come in saying things like, 'I can't eat much anymore. I get full so fast. I think my stomach shrank'. Often, they'll feel full eating only half the amount of food that they used to. No, their stomach didn't physically shrink, but their appetites sure did. That's PERFECT, because if you are eating less but getting more full, you are going to be more likely to keep the weight off.

One young doctor at our clinic had struggled with his weight his whole life but still felt like a slave to food. With my encouragement, he tried an extended fast, although he was nervous knowing his problem with hunger. He said "for the first time in my life I turned down food because I just didn't want it. It wasn't that I was abstaining because I was fasting, I just really wasn't hungry. I've never turned down food like that before." He ate less, but felt more full.

Eater fewer times daily or increasing fasting duration has a twofold benefit. First, you'll decrease your hunger the longer you fast. Second, you'll stop hunger from running your life.

#### Tip — Regular fasting reduces expectations for eating and hunger

#### (Don't) Finish your Plate

We, as adults, like to flatter ourselves by imagining that how much we eat is largely determined our hunger and need for nutrition. That's generally quite untrue. Mostly, we eat depending on the clock, and other external cues like how much is put on our plate.

Children will eat until they are full, no matter how much food is given to them. They listen to their natural hunger and satiety signalling (homeostatic hunger). But as they grow up, we teach them to ignore their body's natural satiety and eat everything on their plate. I've seen many parents chasing their children around the room with forkfuls of food.

Always finishing your plate becomes more problematic as portion sizes have increased since the 1970s. Recent editions of recipes from classic cookbooks like The Joy of Cooking indicate fewer portions per recipe, meaning that each portion is larger than in the past. French fries, hamburgers and sodas are [2–5 times their original sizes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11818300/). The typical cheeseburger in the early 1990s was 4.5 oz and 8 oz today. A bagel measured [3 inches in diameter in the 1990s compared to 6 inches today](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/educational/wecan/news-events/matte1.htm). A bottle of Coke in 1960 was 6.5 ounces, and the 7-Eleven's Double Big Gulp is almost 10 times larger at 64 ounces.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*IaoVvkM8nJbv5nji_XPFRA.jpeg)

If you order a cheeseburger meal, you'll likely finish it all whether today or in the 1990s, due to the expectation effect (you assume a portion is the appropriate size) and pressure to conform to societal norms (finish your plate). Yet you'd be eating more than twice as much to achieve the same level of satiety. As 'Supersized' meals and the 'Big Gulp' drinks became popular, car makers installed larger cup holders, which makes these mega sized portions seem normal.

The difference lies in the perception, not the physiology. Restaurant portions in [Europe are considerably smaller than those in the United States](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10855354/). The perceived norms are culturally determined. People in both France and the United States will usually eat one portion of food, but eat considerably less in France, but feel just as full. The external cue (the empty plate) signifies the end of the meal. People in France are not less hungry at the end of the meal. But they've eaten much less to reduce that hunger and that translates into an overall reduced body weight.

Practically, this might translate into different strategies, such as using smaller plates and portions of food. Therefore, there is very little to stop an American to getting used to a European style portion. You might also skip the appetizer course for the same reasons. People typically eat until the end of the main courses whether they ate an appetizer or not and still feel equally sated.

#### Tip — Manage your expectations. Get used to fewer courses per meal, no snacks between or after meals, smaller plates and portions.

#### Eat Slowly

Special occasions in the Chinese tradition are often celebrated with a 10-course meal that can last for hours. The food takes so long that I am usually full by the end of the 3rd course. It's not just in my mind. It's a conditioned response. Meals typically last a certain length of time — usually 30 minutes or so and once past that, my appetite is shutting down.

Eating slowly decreases ghrelin and increases PYY, which translates into higher ratings of [fullness which reduces future snacking by 25%](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30591684/). Eating quickly leads to higher insulin (bad) and lower levels of satiety hormones PYY, GLP-1 (also bad). [Eating slowly is even more important when eating energy dense foods](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23592679/). People [eating slowly ate fewer calories but felt more full afterwards](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24388483/). Eat less, but feel more full? Sounds like a good strategy to me.
