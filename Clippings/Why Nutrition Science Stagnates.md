## And a Path Forward, Informed by Thomas Kuhn

*This essay is modified from my original submission to Cell Metabolism. The final version is available online with the title* [*Overcoming Impasse in Nutrition Science*](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131\(26\)00013-6)

In his seminal 1962 book [*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*](https://www.lri.fr/~mbl/Stanford/CS477/papers/Kuhn-SSR-2ndEd.pdf), Thomas Kuhn argued that science advances not through the steady accumulation of knowledge, but in stages punctuated by shifts that transform an entire scientific discipline.

Most of the time, scientists work within an accepted worldview or paradigm, with shared tenets, conventions, and experimental methods. This stage, termed *normal science* by Kuhn, “is predicated on the assumption that the scientific community knows what the world is like.” Normal science — aimed at refining and extending the dominant conceptual model rather than challenging its foundations — relies on the community’s resolve to defend the prevailing framework, “if necessary at considerable cost … often suppress\[ing\] fundamental novelties.” Resistance to radical thinking allows scientists to build on one another’s work, specialize, and focus intensely, without being distracted by frequent debate over foundational questions.

Normal science, however, contains the seeds of its own demise. As research explores the contours of a paradigm with increasing precision, discrepancies between expectation and observation inevitably arise, motivating revision of the prevailing model to resolve conflicts. Eventually, anomalies emerge that resist solution, leading to crisis. During this stage, foundational doubts gain legitimacy among groups of scientists, and new models are formulated reflecting a radically different view of the world. The ensuing clash between paradigms either strengthens the existing model or (occasionally) revolutionizes thinking, resulting in adoption of a more powerful and useful model.

My thesis is that paradigm clash tends to stagnate in nutrition research, with inconclusive debates that may persist for decades. Using the conflict between models of obesity pathogenesis as a case study, I examine the roots of scientific impasse and outline a path forward.

**Case Study — Obesity Pathogenesis Models**

*Pre-paradigm (positive energy balance = fat deposition)*

Conceptualization of obesity within nutrition remains partially descriptive rather than anchored to a causal model. For instance, a [2017 Scientific Statement](https://academic.oup.com/edrv/article/38/4/267/3892397) by the Endocrine Society asserts that “Obesity pathogenesis involves … sustained positive energy balance” and a [2022 Standards of Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/45/Supplement_1/S113/138906/8-Obesity-and-Weight-Management-for-the-Prevention) report by the American Diabetes Association affirms, “Behavioral changes that create an energy deficit … will result in weight loss.” Although not necessarily representative of the documents in which they appear, these statements are tautological, revealing a common cognitive trap that impedes causal framing. The physical law of energy conservation dictates that changes in energy balance and body energy stores (with adipose tissue as the primary repository) must be equal. To conceptualize causality, the equation requires a directional arrow that physics itself does not specify.

*Normal science: energy balance model (EBM, positive energy balance* ➡️ *body fat deposition)*

According to the prevailing EBM, “overeating” (energy intake > expenditure) drives weight gain. In the modern environment with an abundance of palatable, energy-dense foods, people tend to consume more energy than they expend, a problem exacerbated by sedentary lifestyles. The excess is stored in adipose tissue, resulting in obesity.

A central precept of the EBM is that, for practical purposes, [all calories are metabolically equivalent](https://academic.oup.com/edrv/article/38/4/267/3892397). Two diets with the same absorbable energy should have identical effects on weight, regardless of macronutrient composition. Consequently, the only way to lose weight is to reduce intake or increase expenditure through physical activity — in other words, “eat less, move more.”

This paradigm has shaped obesity treatment for a century, tracing back to the original calorie-counting methods exemplified by [*Diet and Health: With Key to the Calories*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/15069/15069-h/15069-h.htm), by Lulu Hunt Peters in 1918. Peters lists a variety of foods in 100-kcal portions, ranging from cauliflower to griddle cake. For weight loss, she instructs readers to reduce calories consumed by about 1000 a day (ten portions). “You may eat just what you like — candy, pie, cake, fat meat, butter, cream,” she writes, “but — *count your calories!”* The dieter simply maintains the energy restriction until reaching a normal weight.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*Ij62nAjssRkqpH536T1nJg.png)

Example of the original calorie-counting method to weight control, from Hygeia 1936,14(1):15. Modern versions of this method line the diet section of bookstores today.

However, this approach proved ineffective and potentially harmful. [In a landmark 1959 review](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/562795), Stunkard and McLaren-Hume concluded that results of weight loss programs centered on calorie restriction “are remarkably similar and remarkably poor.” They further cautioned that “such programs may be far from harmless,” citing potential for “emotional disturbances.” This discrepancy between model-based expectation (calorie restriction cures obesity) and observation (the rarity of that outcome) should, according to Kuhn, prompt revisions.

*Model drift*

The low-fat diet of the late twentieth century arguably represents the first major model revision. By targeting dietary fat — the most energy-dense macronutrient — the reasoning went, negative energy balance could be achieved with less difficulty. Echoing the prevailing consensus, a [paper in a leading dietetics journal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9216571/) claimed, “When people are allowed to eat from ranges of high-fat or high-sucrose foods, passive overconsumption only occurs with fat. It follows that fat promotes overconsumption while sucrose probably prevents it.” This version of the EBM culminated in the Food Guide Pyramid of 1992, which placed all fats in the top tier.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*FbxZ7R1HuYmp_23f76vjZw.png)

First Food Guide Pyramid of 1992, promoting a low-fat diet.

Concurrent with this EBM version, obesity prevalence increased rapidly, motivating further revision. [A 2022 version](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916522002428?via%3Dihub=) shifts attention to ultra-processed foods, characterized as “inexpensive, convenient, energy-dense \[items\] … high in portion size, fat, and sugar, and low in protein and fiber,” that act through “food reward, appetite, \[and\] sensory processing” below conscious control.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*O6TVhAj46ocySOmxZtcVrg.png)

A 2022 revision of the EBM focused on ultra-processed foods. From Hall et al. AJCN 2022,115:1243–1254

The [UK Government’s Obesity System Map](https://foresightprojects.blog.gov.uk/2017/10/04/dusting-off-foresights-obesity-report/) describes a complicated array of influences converging on … (look closely in the center) … energy balance. As [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) reportedly said, “A theory that explains everything, explains nothing.”

Beyond utility of these models, [critics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916522005172?via%3Dihub=) have [asked](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41430-022-01179-2):

1. Why has EBM-based dietary strategy failed to stem the obesity epidemic?
2. Why do animals and humans, after experimental overfeeding, typically return to baseline weight?
3. The average U.S. man weighs ~15 kg more today than in 1960. Weight loss of that magnitude triggers intense hunger and reduced energy expenditure, predisposing to regain. Why, then, has the weight level apparently “defended” by these physiological adaptations risen so much among genetically stable populations?
4. Why is obesity prevention so difficult when the energy excess associated with 0.5–1 kg annual weight gain (sufficient to result in obesity by middle age) is only ~15 kcal/d?

Taken together, these potential anomalies have renewed interest in a radical paradigm — one less widely recognized, but with roots as old as the EBM (see [*Good Calories, Bad Calories*](https://garytaubes.com/works/books/good-calories-bad-calories/) by Gary Taubes).

By contrast, EBM proponents regard these concerns not as anomalies, but rather as manifestations of a complex problem requiring further study. After all, “Most anomalies are resolved by normal \[science\] means; most proposals for new theories do prove to be wrong” according to Kuhn.

*Model revolution: carbohydrate-insulin model (CIM, fat deposition* ➡️ *positive energy balance)*

[In the CIM](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916522005172?via%3Dihub=), a high-glycemic load meal elicits hormonal responses (principally an increased ratio of insulin to glucagon in the postprandial state) that shift substrate partitioning toward deposition versus oxidation. Consequently, food intake increases or energy expenditure decreases in the body’s attempt to compensate for the energy sequestered into adipose tissue. This causal direction is consistent with other physiological or pathological states of growth — such as the adolescent growth spurt, fetal development in pregnancy, or enlargement of a massive lipoma — in which deposition of energy into new body tissue drives the positive energy balance, rather than the reverse.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*DNGmKSzGwYY55wfJu3FarA.png)

The first full formulation of the carbohydrate-insulin model. Ludwig et al. AJCN 2021, 114:1873–85

*Paradigm clash paralysis*

Following its formulation, EBM proponents [challenged the CIM](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aav0448), sometimes in premier venues, asserting that its basic tenets had been disproved. Should these critiques be viewed as a valid defense of the prevailing model against a frivolous challenge, or as hyperbole based on unreliable evidence that derails legitimate debate? Numerous [studies and reviews](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=%22carbohydrate-insulin+model%22%5Btiab%5D&sort=date&size=100) have since been published by both sides, typically talking past each other. To explore this breakdown in discourse, consider two highly cited clinical trials, one representing each viewpoint.

In 2018, my collaborators and I published [a parallel-design trial with 5-month diet arms](https://www.bmj.com/content/363/bmj.k4583.long) that reported higher energy expenditure on a low- versus high-carbohydrate diet during weight-loss maintenance. This finding suggests that two 2,000-calorie diets could produce opposite effects on weight trajectory depending on macronutrient composition — contradicting a core premise of the EBM. Soon after, EBM proponents [reanalyzed the publicly available data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41366-019-0456-3) and argued that the reported effect was artifactual, arising from vulnerability of the energy-expenditure method to dietary non-adherence. We responded with evidence indicating that t [he reanalysis was flawed](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41366-019-0466-1) and that our original [findings remain robust](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41430-020-0658-8) in sensitivity analyses. *Which view is correct?*

In 2021, EBM proponents published [a crossover trial with 2-week diet arms](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-020-01209-1) that found higher voluntary energy intake on a low-carbohydrate versus a low-fat diet, a result interpreted as inconsistent with the CIM. [My colleagues and I](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03909-y) argued that an originally unrecognized carry-over effect invalidated the primary findings. [The lead author](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03911-4) countered that, because the primary analyses were pre-specified, alternative analyses could introduce bias and that the original results remain valid despite the carry-over effect. *Which view is correct?*

These studies cost millions of dollars and are unlikely to be soon repeated. The methodological questions appear straightforward, the data are largely public, and definitive answers should be attainable. Given their implications for chronic disease, *why has the field failed to resolve these competing claims?*

**Susceptibility of Nutrition Science to Impasse**

Breakdowns in scholarly discourse appear more the rule than the exception in some areas of nutrition. In the mid-twentieth century, John Yudkin and Ancel Keys [clashed](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK542158/), often with [ad hominem overtones](https://www.independent.ie/life/health-wellbeing/the-man-who-tried-to-warn-us-about-sugar/30032829.html), about whether public health efforts should target sugar or saturated fat. This debate persists today in broadly similar form: sugar, particularly its fructose component, is considered by some [as toxic](https://www.nature.com/articles/482027a) and by others as [merely a source](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00394-016-1345-3) of excess energy; saturated fat is regarded both as a major cause of heart disease and as largely benign. Debate on ultra-processed food and the Nova classification system appears to have polarized into opposing camps that struggle to communicate, [let alone collaborate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394527931_UPFs_Ultra_Polarizing_Foods_Battle_with_Open_Letters_about_NOVA).

Susceptibility to impasse may stem from the great complexity of nutrition science related to diet composition (macronutrients, micronutrients, other bioactive constituents), heterogeneity between people in response to diet, and psycho-behavioral issues affecting dietary adherence. Furthermore, because obesity development is associated with very small daily energy imbalance, studies must often extend for many months to detect effects with reasonable precision — an expensive and logistically difficult undertaking, especially for residential feeding trials. This challenge is compounded by [transient metabolic adaptations](https://www.bmj.com/content/389/bmj-2024-082963) to dietary change that mask chronic effects in shorter studies.

**Overcoming Impasse**

Kuhn’s concept of *incommensurability* suggests that neutrality in paradigm clash is illusory (**Box at bottom**). Without an external vantage point to judge competing models, weighting of criteria like parsimony, plausibility, consistency, explanatory power, and “fruitfulness” (capacity to generate productive lines of inquiry) depends on the paradigm one inhabits. This chasm can be bridged, however, by shared values within the community. Nutrition researchers may disagree on what constitutes scientific truth, but they can agree on precepts of the scientific method in pursuit of that truth.

Because proponents of competing paradigms may use the same terms differently, Kuhn suggests that “participants in a communication breakdown … recognize each other as members of different language communities and then become translators.”That is,each side attempts to imagine how the other would interpret and respond to evidence relevant to the dispute*.* To facilitate this dialogue, all parties must strictly avoid ad hominem attacks, which erode the trust needed to acknowledge error and consider alternative perspectives.

Ultimately, responsibility for resolving paradigm clash rests with the community, according to Kuhn. Scientific disciplines — working through professional societies, ancillary specialties (e.g., statisticians), and funding agencies — must play an active role in defining what counts as reliable evidence and adjudicating conflicting claims. In some cases, existing evidence is sufficient to distinguish between mutually exclusive positions and settle disputes. Where uncertainty persists, their role is to define the studies necessary to resolve the conflict unambiguously and to advocate for the funding to carry them out. Given the importance of nutrition science to public health, efforts to overcome impasse should be prioritized.

> 
> “\[T\]he proponents of competing paradigms practice their trades in different worlds …. Both are looking at the world, and what they look at has not changed.
