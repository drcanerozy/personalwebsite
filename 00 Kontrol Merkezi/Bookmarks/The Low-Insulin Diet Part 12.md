Refined carbohydrates are the bell-bottom jeans of the nutrition world — great in the 1970s, terrible in the 2020s. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, first released in 1977 are more fashion than science. In 1977, white bread is healthy because it is low in fat. By 2020s, the very same white bread had become generally recognized as unhealthy. In 1977, fatty foods like nuts, fatty fish and avocados were deemed unhealthy. Dietary fat causes heart disease, don’t you know? By the 2020s, the very same nuts, fatty fish and avocados are considered good-for-you ‘super-foods’. Healthy fats prevent heart disease, don’t you know?

The mistakes did not stop there. The Dietary Guidelines focused obsessively on nutrients (fat, protein, calories) rather than foods (cabbage, beef, eggs). This reductionist thinking imagines that all nutrients are the same. A calorie is a calorie, whether from cookies or broccoli. All carbohydrates are the same, whether from sugary cereal or bean salad. This is absurd, although repeated often enough, started to seem logical. Imagine saying that all human beings are mostly carbon and so is charcoal, so replacing Brad Pitt with a lump of charcoal is roughly the same. All calories are not the same. All carbohydrates are not the same.

Like those bell-bottoms, the dietary advice rooted in the 1970s did not age well and brought unintended consequences. Eating all those refined carbs spiked the glycemic [load (GL) of the average](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10954760/) …

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:288/1*jGlDG7hts8RfO5KI-pTMIg.jpeg)

Plants store excess energy from photosynthesis in the form of carbohydrates, and animals store the excess as fat. This is why plant heavy diets typically contain more carbohydrates, which stimulate the greatest insulin surge. Eating fewer total carbohydrates can certainly reduce insulin, but that not the only way. Digestion and absorption can modify this hormonal response, and therefore weight gain.

However, many traditional diets are based on carbohydrates, yet people still avoided obesity. The Irish and Germans traditionally love their potatoes. The Asians traditionally loved their rice. The Italians loved their pasta. The key is to lower insulin levels, which is not the same as eating less carbohydrates.

Carbohydrates are sugar. There are many types of sugar, which usually ends in the suffix -ose, such as glucose, fructose, lactose and sucrose (table sugar). The human body mostly uses glucose. Blood glucose is also known as blood sugar. Starches (wheat, rice, oats etc.) are long chains of glucose arranged in a structure called amylopectin (about 70%) and amylose (about 30%). Starches are mostly plant based because plants store energy from photosynthesis as sugars, just as body fat is the way animals store energy (calories). Sugars usually only contain single sugars (glucose, fructose) or two sugars (sucrose, lactose).

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*n2MZclLmB60ldI6ZdVWONQ.png)

Carbohydrates used to be classified as a ‘simple carbohydrate’ if it contained 2 or fewer sugars in a chain and a ‘complex carbohydrate’ if it was longer, based on the incorrect assumption that complex carbohydrates are digested and absorbed slowly and simple carbohydrates quickly. But it is not true, so this classification system is as useless as a wet newspaper. For example, white bread, a ‘complex’ carbohydrate’ spikes blood glucose higher and faster than table sugar.

The modern classification of starches divides them as:

> 1\. Rapidly Digestible Starch (RDS) — releases glucose within 20 minutes — Bad
> 
> 2\. Slowly Digestible Starch (SDS) — releases glucose within 20–120 minutes — Better
> 
> 3\. Resistant Starch (RS) — cannot be digested — Best

The Glycemic Index (GI) or the related Glycemic Load (GL) measure how quickly blood glucose rises on a scale of 0 to 100. A higher GI means that your blood glucose rises higher and more quickly, which causes higher insulin levels. The higher the insulin, the more weight is gained. The total number of carbohydrates is an important factor in weight gain, but how quickly blood glucose spikes is also critical. This depends on the speed of digestion and absorption.

## Speed of Digestion

The standard paradigm of metabolism suggest that we eat food, which contains calories and other macronutrients, which leads to weight gain. This is not even close to the whole picture. Each step along the metabolic pathway can influence weight gain.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:595/1*8WNa9oZRYaNA4sJpYxT8gA.jpeg)

Instead, a more complete and correct physiologic sequence from foods to metabolism looks like this:

> 1\. **Food** — Contains macronutrients such as carbohydrates, proteins and fats. The total food energy contained may be measured in calories.
> 
> 2\. **Digestion** — Food must be broken down into a form that allows our body to absorb its nutrients. Refining, cooking, chewing, blending, churning by the stomach, type of starch, are all examples of processes that affect digestion.
> 
> 3\. **Absorption** — Cells in the intestines absorb the nutrients and pass them into the bloodstream. Grinding and refining are examples of processes that affect absorption.
> 
> 4\. **Hormones** — Different hormones are released in response to absorbed nutrients such as insulin, GLP-1, GIP, Peptide YY, cholecystokinin and others.
> 
> 5\. **Weight Gain** — Hormones set the Body Fat Thermostat and gives instructions to either gain or lose weight.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*2hbnaneY9N08bkNfpYhmng.jpeg)

The stomach begins digestion by mechanically churning the food and mixing it with stomach acid. It also acts as a reservoir, since we eat food faster than we can absorb the contained nutrients. To address this mismatch, the stomach holds the foods and releases a little bit at a time into the small intestines where it gets absorbed into the bloodstream. If the stomach empties quickly, blood glucose spikes more quickly, which increases the insulin effect. When food particles are smaller and softer, the stomach empties faster.

After we eat the starch (long chain of glucose), it must be digested into smaller pieces for absorption. This is done both mechanically, by chewing and the churning action of the stomach, and chemically through enzymes called amylases present in the saliva and pancreatic fluid and stomach acid. Once digested into single glucose molecules, they can be absorbed through the intestines into the bloodstream. Because starches are glucose, blood glucose (blood sugar) rises.

Natural carbohydrates are often contained within a physical barrier that resists digestion, such as an intact plant cell wall (whole grains, steel cut oats). They are almost always mixed with an indigestible type of carbohydrate, fiber. For example, rice is naturally covered by a tough outer husk. Whole wheat grains are protected by a tough outer shell of bran. The intact plant cell wall, large particle size and fiber slows the rate of digestion and absorption. Blood glucose rises more slowly and therefore insulin levels also rise more slowly. Less insulin = less weight gain.

Refined carbohydrates are digested and absorbed much, much more quickly and easily. During refining of wheat, the bran, germ and oils that slow digestion is removed during processing. Then it is machine ground into flour, a fine dust which requires no chewing. The husk of the rice is removed with the inner bran and polished into white rice.

Refining changes all carbohydrates into rapidly digestible starches by ‘pre-digestion’. This allows very quick absorption of glucose, leading to a massive spike in blood glucose and a massive spike in insulin. Does this matter? Definitely. More insulin = more weight gain.

## Tip — Eat less refined (starchy) carbohydrates

A recent large study revealed that eating starchy, refined carbohydrates like bread, potatoes and rice was associated with a [2.6 kg (5.7 pounds) weight gain over 4 years. Extrapolating over the 20 years, eating more potatoes is associated with an average 22-pound weight gain](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37758268/). But not all carbohydrates are the same.

Eating non-starchy carbohydrates, like broccoli, cabbage and zucchini, was associated with a 3kg (6.6 pounds) weight *loss*. The type of carbohydrate (natural versus refined and processed) makes a big difference. Grandma was right. You should eat your veggies, which doesn’t include French fries.

## Fast vs Slow Carbs (Glycemic Index)

How quickly carbohydrates are digested and absorbed can be measured by the GI. And it can make a huge difference. Consider three different breakfasts with identical calories.

1\. Low GI — Vegetable Omelet — 40% carbs

2\. Medium GI — Steel cut Oatmeal — 64% carbs

3\. High GI — Instant Oatmeal — 64% carbs

Steel cut oats preserve the structure (cell wall) of the oat kernel, making digestion and absorption of the carbohydrate contained within very slow. Instant oats are ‘pre-digested’, having been pulverized to a fine dust for quick cooking, and the glucose is absorbed quickly. Both contain the same number of calories, carbohydrates and are even the same food. The difference lies only in the level of processing.

Does this matter? Oh, yes. The [total glucose effect of the instant oats was almost **4 times** higher than the omelet, and **almost double** that of the steel cut oats](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10049982/). But there’s more bad news. After breakfast, the instant oats group subsequently ate **81%** more calories than the omelet group and **53%** more calories than the steel cut oats group. Ouch.

But you already knew this. Eat a vegetable omelet for breakfast, and you’re full until lunch. Eat highly refined carbohydrates and you’re hungry by the mid-morning. The most important factor is not the calories. Eating fewer carbohydrates means lower blood glucose and less insulin. Non carbohydrate containing foods generally have a GI and GL of zero. It’s quite simple, really — if you eat glucose or chains of glucose, your blood glucose goes up. If you don’t eat glucose, then your blood glucose does **not** go up.

The most important factor is not even the carbohydrates, but the speed that nutrients are digested and absorbed, which influences the insulin response. Eating refined carbohydrates elicits a very different insulin response compared to less processed carbohydrates, which translates into very different eating behavior. In a similar study, [kids who ate cornflakes and bananas (high GL) versus eggs and strawberries (Low GL) for breakfast ate **65%** more calories at lunch](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21070678/). 65%!

Consider what is happening in the body. The breakfast of ‘pre-digested’ instant oats massively spikes insulin, which orders your body to “Please store these calories”. The violence of the insulin spike pushes most of the glucose (and calories) into storage (body fat), leaving little energy for basic metabolism — body heat generation, brain, heart, liver, kidney etc. Blood glucose crashes about 2 hours after the instant oats, making you hypoglycemic. Hunger surges, sending you out looking for more food. With the omelet breakfast, insulin doesn’t go up much, so fewer calories are stored (body fat), leaving most of them available for metabolism. You stay fuller for longer and eat less at the next meal.

## Tip — Choose foods with a low Glycemic Index (GI) and Glycemic Load (GL). Avoid rapidly digested starches.

When choosing bread, look for stone ground flour, which leaves particles larger than machine ground floud and slows digestion. Both pumpernickel (GI 45) and rye (GI 65) bread have substantially lower than GI than white bread (95). Rye flour is high in fiber, and traditional pumpernickel is made with coarsely ground rye flour with a sourdough starter. The fermentation process of sourdough generates lactic acid which reduces the glucose effect, as we will discuss later in the book.

## Amylopectin versus Amylose

Starches are chains of glucose arranged either as amylopectin or amylose. Amylopectin is highly branched and easily dissolved in water, making it more easily digested by amylases. The glucose in amylose is arranged in a linear, non-branched helical structure. The [compact packing and layered crystalline structure resists amylase digestion so the glucose is released more slowly, lowering its insulin effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7840071/). Eating starches with more amylose reduces the insulin effect, which is beneficial for weight loss.

Rice is a globally vital food source, eaten by over 2/3 of the world’s population. After harvest, the husk is removed from the rice, leaving brown rice. This is polished to white rice, removing the bran with its oils, vitamins and minerals. [Different type of rice have different concentrations of amylose](https://www.ijpab.com/vol6-iss2a182.php). Rice varieties with higher amylopectin tend to be stickier when cooked. Short grain rice, often used in sushi is low in amylose (10–19%) and sticky or glutinous rice has almost no amylose. This results in sticky rice having the highest GI at 98. The more common fluffy rice, jasmine and basmati contain more amylose (25–30%) and therefore sport a lower GI of 58. Converted, or par-boiled rice has the lowest GI (38) because it is partially cooked with the husk on and then cooled. [Eating the entire grain of rice slows digestion time.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6364775/)

Quinoa, beans, lentils and oats are also good sources of amylose. Arrowroot powder, used for thickening contains almost 30% amylose and may be a better choice than corn starch. Baby potatoes have higher levels of amylose compared to regular potatoes, so may be a better choice, although the difference is very slight.

There are different types of Amylopectin — A, B and C. Wheat products contain mostly type A amylopectin, which is the most easily digestible. Beans are a good source of amylopectin C which is the hardest to digest. The undigested starch is not absorbed into the body and delivered to the colon, where it ferments and forms gas, often causing flatulence (farts) and hysterical laughter from adolescent boys and most grown men, too. Amylopectin B is intermediate and found in potatoes and bananas.

## Tip — Choose higher amylose starches. Choose foods higher in Amylopectin C (best) or B (OK) rather than type A.

## Resistant Starch

Resistant starches contain carbohydrates but are not absorbed by the body. The classic example is fiber. Since fiber cannot be digested, no glucose can be absorbed, and insulin doesn’t go up. But fiber may provide some hidden satiety benefits. Fiber increases bulk, which increases satiety through distention of the stomach (discussed later in the book). Fiber passes through the intestines to the colon, where it is fermented to Short Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, butyrate and proprionate. These [SCFAs increase secretion of satiety hormones such as GLP1 and Peptide YY.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18796545/)

Some good sources of fermentable fibers includes apples, almonds, avocados, pears, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, carrots, grapefruit, whole oats, beans, lentils, peas, chickpeas, chia seed and oranges. Chia seed is a particularly good ‘hack’ because it can be incorporated into many drinks without loss of taste. Chia seeds also forms a viscous layer that partially blocks glucose absorption and increase satiety.

## Tip- Eat More Fiber

You can increase the resistant starch in rice by cooking and then cooling it. During cooking, starches gelatinize (become more liquid like) and when cooled, the starch reconnects forms a new crystal structure in a process called retrogradation. That’s why cold rice is brittle and hard to eat. [The new partially crystallized structure, called retrograded resistant starch is less susceptible to digestion by amylase](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32959735/). This slows digestion, gastric emptying rate and glycemic index, which increases satiety and decreases the glucose and insulin effect. [Cooking, refrigerating then reheating rice increases the resistant starch by almost 2.5-fold](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26693746/). Resistant starches increase the longer [the rice is cooled, even up to 7 day](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S073352100500144X) s.

This does not work for potatoes, however. The resistant starch generated on cooking and cooling is reversed upon reheating. To maximize resistant starches in potatoes, eat them cold, where resistant starch content is increased by 57%. A c [old potato salad with vinegar reduces the glycemic index by 43% and the insulin index by 31% compared to fresh potatoes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16034360/).Less insulin = less weight gain.

**Tip — Eat more Resistant Starches. Cook, cool and reheat rice. Eat cooked potatoes cold.**

## The Food Matrix

Even though both pasta (GI 50) and white bread (GI 75) are made from highly refined carbohydrates (flour), the glycemic index varies substantially, due to the difference in the food matrix. This is the physical structure in which the nutrients are embedded, which impacts the speed of digestion. The starch granules in cooked pasta are mixed with the main protein, gluten. Surrounded by the coagulated protein network, the [starches become harder to digest and the glucose and insulin effect is about 40% lower than bread](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Yvonne-Granfeldt/publication/21372713_On_the_importance_of_processing_conditions_product_thickness_and_egg_addition_for_the_glycaemic_and_hormonal_responses_to_pasta_A_comparison_with_bread_made_from_'pasta_ingredients'/links/568a560f08ae051f9afa4332/On-the-importance-of-processing-conditions-product-thickness-and-egg-addition-for-the-glycaemic-and-hormonal-responses-to-pasta-A-comparison-with-bread-made-from-pasta-ingredients.pdf), Slower digestion = slower rise in glucose = lower glycemic index.

The difference in food matrix also means that the peak insulin effect of [parboiled rice with tomato sauce is about 45% lower than white bread](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10822284/). The amylose in the rice complexes with the fats in the tomato sauce to form amylose-lipid complex that resist digestion and are heat stable about 100 Celsius. Less insulin = less weight gain.

**Tip-Pasta and low glycemic rice are better choices than white bread due to the food matrix.**

Oats are high in carbohydrates but can be useful for weight loss. They are relatively high in protein (14%) compared to other grains and contains significant (4%) beta glucans, which raises viscosity. The [higher viscosity slows down the emptying of the stomach, which slows the rate of digestion and glucose release](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2475299123081957). Oat starch contains 25–30% amylose and can complex with fats to form resistant starch, which further lowers it glucose and insulin effect. Slower digestion = lower insulin = less weight gain.

Steel cut oats, also called Irish oatmeal, are whole oat kernels cut with steel blades into pin-head sized pieces. They have lots of fiber and are minimally processed, so they must be cooked for approximately 40 minutes with plenty of liquid to be edible. Even then, steel cut oats are digested very slowly, which means they have the lowest insulin effect.

The most popular oats are rolled oats, which have a glycemic index higher than steel cut oats, but lower than instant. Rolled oats are steamed, flattened between rollers, and then dried in a kiln, which increases the amylose content. The thickness of the oats also affects the speed of digestion. Thinner oats, sometimes called ‘quick oats’ are more easily digested, and therefore a higher glycemic index.

Instant oatmeal is steamed longer, flattened more, and then ground into the tiniest of pieces, allowing them to be cooked under 2 minutes, usually just by adding hot water. This processing means these ‘pre-digested’ oats are quickly absorbed leading to a high glycemic index. Quick digestion = Blood Glucose spike = More insulin = More weight gain.

Oat bran is made from grinding oat grain and separating out the bran. It has 5.5% beta glucan and lots of dietary fiber. Adding oat bran to foods can significantly reduce glycemic index due to its high dietary fiber.

**Tip - Eat steel cut, not instant oats.**
