Do you need to eat to live? Most people would say yes. But there is a small group of people—self-proclaimed “Breatharians”—who might say, “no, not necessarily.”

Though the fringe philosophy has existed in its modern incarnation since at least the 1970s, and is modeled after religious ascetics of old, I hadn’t heard of Breatharians until a *New York Post* article that went viral in early June. The title, like most tabloid headlines, is a complete sentence that reads like a factual, vetted discovery: [“Breatharian couple survives on ‘the universe’s energy’ instead of food.”](http://nypost.com/2017/06/15/breatharian-couple-survives-on-the-universes-energy-instead-of-food/) That couple, a life-coaching duo named Akahi Ricardo and Camila Castillo, belong to a community of people who believe you don’t need food to live, that surviving on non-food substances like sunlight, air, and “prana” allows people to live more evolved, healthy, spiritual lives.

I poke around the Internet and discover Ricardo and Castillo’s website. I learn that they spent years as fire dancers and performers in South America. I find a [Snopes page](http://www.snopes.com/breatharians/) debunking their claims. I learn that there are multiple deaths associated with following Breatharianism, and several infamous Breatharian gurus.

I get morbidly obsessed [with their videos](http://breatharianschool.com/). Ricardo is zealous, almost panting from exertion in some of his long rants. The best optics come when Castillo is at his side, looking beatific and occasionally chiming in. The clips have buzzy names like, “How to transform problems into opportunities.” But beneath every inspirational message is the thrumming question. *Are these people really not eating?*

Ricardo and Castillo’s website says they are hosting a retreat in California. I e-mail them and ask if I can join.

---

I am a California native. I have been to drum circles, visited psychics, participated in Cacao ceremonies. I’m no stranger to the various incarnations of woo. And to be honest, I’m curious about these people. They’ve got to be wrong or lying. But what if they’re not? What if they’ve stumbled on some secret that could help every human on Earth thumb their nose at the basic rules of mortality?

At the retreat, six people from around the U.S. will stay in a rented house together. They’re a ragtag group, and they lack obvious commonalities aside from an interest in spiritual development and a suspicion that food is holding them back. None appears wildly rich or eccentric. Most have “normal” jobs, families, and personal lives, filled with the same unpredictable successes and failures as everyone else. But they’re looking for a trap door. Breatharianism, they hope, is a hack to the human condition.

All of the retreat-goers intend to complete the suggested fast: no food or water for three days, and only juice for another four. Going without water is something that most doctors consider dangerous, but for Breatharians, an “initiation” fast is essential to beginning a life where food is optional. There will be no doctors present at the retreat, and the only thing remotely resembling medical oversight will be a white box called a Bio-Well that supposedly measures the human energy field and [chakra alignment](http://www.bio-well.eu/), and that ends up having broken in transit on its way to the retreat.

This 8-Day Process, as Ricardo has coined it, seems to be in demand. Ricardo is flying in from Costa Rica, where he just finished doing the same retreat (priced at $1,080 per person) with fourteen people, and will fly to Poland to lead a 20-person retreat afterward.

Ricardo’s “process," which he’s agreed to let me drop in on, is supposed to be a gentler, safer version of the better-known 21-day Breatharian process, which has been blamed for multiple deaths. It dangerously excludes both food and water for one week, followed by two weeks of juice or water fasting.

Later, I ask Ricardo, *Why do this? Why give up a central part of human life, go against medical advice, when there are so many spiritual practices out there that don’t require you to give up food?* His answer surprises me. “When I started this, I felt cleaner, I felt sharper,” he tells me, pausing. “I felt…fearless.”

On day two, when Ricardo has given me permission to visit, I’m driving through rolling vineyards into the town of Guerneville, where the retreat is being held. It’s an open-minded town in an open-minded state, a shabby-chic blip of a place on the winding Russian River, popular for several gourmet restaurants, gay pool parties, used-furniture stores, and river-rafting companies. On weekends, sunburned weekenders flip-flop their way down the main road, crushed beer cans, Ziplocked car keys, and deflated inner tubes in their arms. As they shrink in my rearview mirror, I feel like I’m crossing into a different world. What, I wonder, does a person’s days look like when they reject breakfast, lunch, and dinner?

When I knock on the door of the tree-wreathed cabin, a middle-aged woman with wide eyes opens it, and I glimpse a sparsely populated living room where people are staring at me. It’s clear to me by the strained silence that none of the participants knew I was coming here. Of the six people filtering in and out of the living room, there are four women and two men. Everyone’s white, with the exception of the woman who opened the door. The youngest look like they’re in their early 30s, the oldest, their early 60s. One of the women is Argentine, one is originally from Madagascar, and another is Bulgarian, though they all live in the U.S. The other three are American.

Someone mentions how beautiful it is outside, and I hear Ricardo remark that, after becoming so attuned to the air, he can see the individual particles of light energy floating over the nearby valley.

“You’ll be able to see them soon, too,” he tells the participants. They nod and move on before I can ask, “Really? How?” Later, when I ask one of them if he was serious, she’ll say, “...We all tend to exaggerate things, I guess.”

Yoga mats have been spread on the floor, cushions arranged as the group gathers for a breathing exercise, one of many that will provide energetic succor in the days ahead. It’s one of only two daily activities that the $1,700 retreat includes. There is one guided breathing exercise at 9 A.M. and a second at 5 P.M. The rest of the time is activity-free. Since people won’t have much energy anyway, this is less of a problem than it might be otherwise.

Everyone has been fasting on juice since they arrived yesterday. Tonight at 8 P.M., they’ll take their last sips of water and turn in for the three-day dry fast. “I am worried, because I don’t know how my body is going to react,” a yoga-studio owner and former comedian named Tina tells me. (The names of all the participants have been changed to protect their privacy.)

One of the men, a realtor from Los Angeles with a deep tan and slick, shaggy brown hair, asks the others if he and his girlfriend Lori can use the bathroom for a while. They want to do a coffee enema to prep for tomorrow. “We have lots of tea tree oil for the bathroom,” he says.

All of this talk of enemas and controlled starvation has me really wondering why anyone would try Breatharianism. But when I see it as an offshoot of the out-of-control wellness industry, it makes more sense. Like many dubious methods, it has a seed of legitimate science at its core. In 2016, for instance, a Japanese scientist named Yoshinori Ohsumi won the Nobel Prize in Medicine for his research on fasting and the way it kickstarts a regenerative cellular recycling process called “autophagy,” Greek for “self-eating.” His discoveries gave extra credence to long-held traditions of fasting from around the world and modern techniques that use it as a way to jumpstart healing in the body.

It’s relatively simple to do moderate fasts on your own, but we live in a world of excess, where everything becomes, at some point, a product for purchase and consumption, science be damned. We live in a world where the [GOOP health summit exists](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2017/jun/14/gwyneth-glows-like-a-radioactive-swan-my-day-at-the-goop-festival), where people pay $220 to be poked with [“immune support” IV drips](http://www.dripdoctors.com/limitless-drip.php) or seek the fountain of youth in [unproven juice blends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MonaVie) and exotic supplements. Meanwhile, basic American health care, which was overpriced to begin with, has become a political volleyball and most of us [don’t have enough savings](https://www.forbes.com/sites/niallmccarthy/2016/09/23/survey-69-of-americans-have-less-than-1000-in-savings-infographic/#269bf5601ae6) to cover the cost of a serious accident or illness. Put together the uncertainty of basic health care and our willingness to look for it in odd places, and it lands us here. Breatharianism promises wellness, mental and physical, for less than you’re doing, less than you’re eating, less than you’re paying now—after the initial investment, at least.

I sit down with the retreat-goers and ask them why they came. They tell me that they just want what everyone wants: health and healing, both of body and mind.

Mary, the woman who opened the door for me, says she wants to grow spiritually. Then she confides in me that she suffers terrible acid reflux every time she eats, despite having eliminated meat, cheese, cooked food, and gluten from her diet. She’s at the end of her rope.

“I’m getting tired of it. So now I’m going to be Breatharian,” she says earnestly. Later Mary tells me that her husband has ALS. The diagnosis has been spiritually and logistically devastating for them both. She’s desperate for something, anything resembling a treatment or cure for him, too.

Her hope doesn’t come out of nowhere. The Breatharians hint that they have powers of energetic healing. When Castillo visits the retreat, she talks about curing herself from serious uterine pain and tells the stories of women healed from cysts, infertility, and painful periods after working with her.

Tina, who’s the wryest and most skeptical of the attendees, said she’s here for several reasons, but admits that one of them is a desire to slim down and “break her addiction to food... If anyone tells you they don’t want to lose weight here, they’re probably lying." It stokes my concerns that at least part of this retreat is rooted in group-sanctioned anorexia.

At 5 P.M., it’s meditation hour and we sit down in the living room. Everyone gets comfy and closes their eyes, and Ricardo begins to lead the breathing lesson, the words taking shape with Ecuadorian flourish. We’re taking deep inhales and he asks us to “imprint the infinite symbol onto every single one of your cells,” and I roll my closed eyes, because single-batch cellular branding seems like overkill, or at least something that could take many hours. As I’m pondering this, Ricardo leads the breathing to a fever pitch. The room sounds like the soundtrack of a B horror movie, where everyone’s running from a killer in the woods.

When I leave the house and close the door behind me that day, I’m relieved to be back in the normal world. There’s something overwhelmingly intimate about the closeness of the house, the people in their pajamas doing their enemas, purging their toxins, divulging their deep secrets. I drive back home through the fading light and, exhausted, go to bed without eating dinner.

---

Driving back to the Breatharian house two days later, I feel a mounting sense of dread. It’s been two and a half days since the participants had food or water and I can’t stop thinking about Verity Linn, [the woman who died](http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/scotland/453661.stm) emaciated and alone on a remote shore in the Scottish Highlands in 1999. Among her belongings was a diary that chronicled her food- and water-free “spiritual cleanse” and a copy of the book *Living on Light*, written by preeminent Breatharian Ellen “Jasmuheen” Greve.

It’s tricky to pin down just how much Breatharians eat once they finish their initiation process, and that seems to be by design. In the *Post* article from June, Castillo was quoted as saying that she and Ricardo spent years eating “nothing,” including during her pregnancy. In other articles, and in person, it becomes clear that “nothing” still includes fruit and vegetable juice—still very little, but definitely not nothing.

Breatharian leaders have oft been undressed in public. Jasmuheen was humiliated by a *60 Minutes* episode in which she started to fail and slur during a monitored dry fast. And early Breatharian leader Wiley Brooks, who claimed that all foods and liquids are poisonous, saw many of his followers dispatch in 1983 after he was spotted grabbing takeout, including, allegedly, a chicken pot pie and biscuits at a hotel and groceries from 7-Eleven. Since then, he’s gone so off the rails as to claim that McDonald’s’ double quarter-pounders are the best and only food to eat, because they’re [“not radioactive.”](https://www.vice.com/en_us/article/kwpzwm/breatharian-leader-wiley-brooks-lives-on-light-air-and-quarter-pounders)

When I knock on the retreat house door, though, and Mary answers again, her eyes are bright. She’s alive—very alive, in fact, and says she’s feeling good.

As I sit down, Tina walks over to the refrigerator, grabs a bottle of water, takes a swig, then spits it out in the kitchen sink. “This is how we drink water now,” she jokes dryly. Just a quick splash to prevent the dry mouth that's setting in.

She and the others say they’re happy with the retreat, though it seems a bit light on activities to me. Aside from the morning and evening meditations, Ricardo isn’t around. Everyone just hangs out in the dark, stuffy house from 10:30 A.M. to 5 P.M. enduring the fast, maybe with the occasional trip to the river or to gather apples and blackberries that they cannot, at present, eat. The kitchen table is littered with drawings and watercolors of numinous images. The strange figures, geometric patterns, and nature scenes look like something from a drug trip.

But they tell me that the retreat feels worth it.

“I had to delay my house payment to afford this,” Mary says. “But who cares? This is more important.”

Beth, a school-district accountant who says she came to get to the “next level” spiritually, is also very satisfied. “The price of this retreat is nothing for what he gives you. I mean, he’s giving you the universe. Who else can give you that?” she says.

Even the couple from L.A. seems to be having a good time. They’re feeling renewed now that their bodies are recycling the old water in their systems—water that was supposedly carrying toxic “information” about past negative experiences. (The foundation of this idea, likely based on an experiment involving rice submerged in water, has been [widely criticized](https://www.csicop.org/specialarticles/show/a_grain_of_truth_recreating_dr._emotos_rice_experiment#_ftn1).)

The problem isn’t the food and water themselves, they tell me, relaying Ricardo’s Breatharian teachings, it’s the way most people consume them, full of thoughtlessness and unrestrained animal need. Without both those things, the participants say they’re starting to feel better, more in control. Fundamental consumption, for now, appears to be a choice.

But I have to wonder, is all this inner peace just a symptom of starvation?

Dr. Perry G. Fine, a professor of anesthesiology at the University of Utah, has worked with terminally ill patients who decide to stop eating as a way of peacefully ending their lives. “Patients have oftentimes described themselves as entering a transcendental, blissful, or euphoric state after they stop eating,” he tells me, though pain-killing drugs exaggerate the phenomenon in some. “But that doesn’t mean it’s a positive thing if the brain is being starved of nutrition and hydration. You may have a sense of well-being, but you might be doing harm to yourself,” he says.

Even cravings seem to be less than expected, although Mary slips into a five-minute reverie about pho. “I really want to get that when I get home,” she tells the others. “Just order a big vegan pho...” She trails off, and her face looks far away. Then she snaps back and seems to remember where we are. “Without the noodles, of course,” she clarifies. “Just the broth.”

That evening, after the intense breathing exercise—which this time sounds less like a horror movie and more like porn—it’s finally time to reintroduce liquids. Everyone crowds around the blender like it’s an aid truck in a disaster area, angling for a look at the apples, which the L.A. couple picked nearby and are about to be blended, strained, and cut with water. When the juices (both apple and grape) are finished, they’re poured into two stainless steel bowls. Lori carries them to the table with both hands in a way that feels sacred.

Ricardo and Castillo make sure everyone has a glass, including me. I want to pass, but it seems rude, especially with the wild way everyone is staring at the juice. There’s something unnerving about being this connected to the bodies of complete strangers, knowing that the fluid is essential to their lives, imagining the way it will cascade down their dry digestive tracks and flood their parched kidneys.

With soupy, cloudy liquid clasped in their hands, everyone repeats a blessing, hoping that this juice will replace the “bad” water that has now been passed out of their bodies. Then they begin to slurp it up. It’s a reverent, awkward moment, these fluid-starved devotees rediscovering the radical, wet, delicious—no, miraculous—juice, as if for the first time. They’re lost in it.

When I take a sip, it tastes watery. I feel uncomfortably close to everyone in the room. “It’s so flavorful,” someone remarks.

Once, in my early 20s, I had a tutoring gig with a girl from a rich family. They had a cook, with whom I’d often hang out after the evening lesson was over. She was the one who taught me the phrase “Hunger is the best sauce.” Scarcity and deprivation, in other words, change our palate. From one perspective, you could say, we finally taste what’s there. From another, we dramatically lower our standards.

On the final day of the retreat, Ricardo leads a lesson in energy healing on the porch and asks one of the retreat-goers to lie on a massage table before him. He demonstrates how to harness “universal energy,” turning his right finger in front of his left palm like he’s spinning cotton candy onto a stick, then aiming it at those who get supine before him.

Then they ask me to get on the table. I don’t want to be rude, so I climb up and lie on my back, feeling unpleasantly exposed. I’m an un-ascendent pagan with a stomach full of eggs and toast on the spiritual operating table.

I stare at the clouds, my hands crossed over my chest. Eventually I close my eyes. Against my better judgment, I relax and feel a strange sense of calm coming over me. It’s either working, or my body is happy to be horizontal after a restless night of sleep. Either way, it’s a blissful break from my internal monologue of pseudoscience fact-checking. A few minutes later, I feel an arm resting softly on my shoulder. When I open my eyes, it’s Ricardo. “We’re finished,” he whispers.

This part doesn’t seem so foreign to me. Reiki and other kinds of energy healing are popular where I live in San Francisco. But I’m not clear why Ricardo is qualified to teach it for money, since he tells me that he hasn’t studied this under anyone else. In fact, apart from some light research on Breatharianism, Ricardo’s method is something he developed without much, if any, formal background or oversight.

As I’m mulling this, Beth pulls me aside to tell me that all my chi is up in my brain, leaving my lower chakras to languish in energetic drought.

“Remember,” she tells me as she points to her skull, “this is the PC”—she lowers her hands to her heart—“but this is the Internet.”

Ricardo encourages everyone to practice healing on their own while he and Castillo sit down with me to talk about Breatharianism.

They were living in rural Bolivia, where they’d set up a community arts center for kids, when they underwent the 21-day process after a friend and neighbor did it. When it was over, they just kept juicing because normal eating seemed like overkill. By that time, they’d already been living on very little for years. Some days, they’d subsist on nothing but oranges and homemade snacks with the maca (a Peruvian herb), seeds, and honey, they say.

“When we would try to eat again after the fast, even one little grape felt like so much work,” Ricardo says. That went on for several years.

Now that they have two kids, the couple eats normal food more often. In the past two days, they say, they had noodle soup and cream of broccoli soup with them. And they eat food on special occasions. On a recent trip to Paris, they even ate fondue—which sounds great but is also a bit like saying they did speed after leading an NA meeting, and not something they seem to be emphasizing with the people who paid to be here.

“It’s a shame everyone focuses so much on the food,” they tell me, adding that spirituality doesn’t have to be so extreme. They confide they’re thinking of dropping the term “Breatharians,” since they don’t identify with people like Jasmuheen and they don’t like to encourage people to stop eating at their retreats. But on the other hand, they like the name “Breatharian” because they see breath as a type of food.

To their credit, Ricardo and Castillo could be worse when it comes to New Age spiritual leaders. They often tell the participants some version of “you can do anything we can do.” They aren’t flaunting ostentatious jewelry or brand-name clothing, and they’ll admit to eating when pressed. But as former fire dancers and self-proclaimed performance artists, they also understand the value of theater.

They often fuel rumors that they don’t eat by omitting the fact that they *do eat*, if not much. Savvy spiritual leaders have long known that perpetuating the myth that you’re superhuman is a great way to get followers. But it seems disingenuous to lure people in with the promise of a food-free life when you’re enjoying French cheese, even if occasionally.

“I think they eat a lot less than you might think they do,” Tina replies when I tell her that Ricardo and Castillo do eat. If you took away the death-defying fasting, would anyone have signed up for this retreat? I later ask the six. Four of them write back and say probably not, and that they were attracted to the couple because they’re Breatharians.

I hug the retreat-goers and, on my way out, ask Mary how she’s doing.

“I feel very at peace,” she tells me. “I know what to do now, and I know it’s going to be okay,” and for a moment, my heart breaks for her and I really hope she’s right.

According to accounts of people who have [survived near-death hypothermia](https://www.outsideonline.com/2152131/freezing-death) and the [doctors who have revived them](http://www.ktre.com/story/5833977/climber-died-in-a-state-of-bliss), there’s a kind of peaceful quiet, a hallucinatory calm that surrounds some people before they freeze to death. Extreme cold slows blood flow, brain function, heartbeat, and metabolism. A narcotic sedation can settle in, a letting go. Because at some point, everyone gets weary of fighting to stay alive.

Eating, like staying warm, is one of those things most of us take for granted. Is there a more frequent reminder of our mortality than the growl of the stomach, the plummeting blood sugar, the rampant search for a good restaurant, a grocery store, a takeout place that’s still open?

Who doesn’t tire of *wake-eat-work-eat-exercise-eat-sleep, repeat* sometimes, even when it’s going right? Keeping yourself alive day to day can be exhausting. Follow Breatharianism and there’s a promise you’ll abbreviate the mundane work of it. Breatharianism says “no thanks” to the food rat race, thumbs its nose at science.

A lot of people I spoke to about the Breatharians said a life without food hardly seemed worth living to them. But for some, life gets to the point where it is barely worth living anyway. If something promises relief, joy, health—food doesn’t seem so hard to give up. That, and it’s a comfort to imagine a universe like the one the Breatharians promise. It’s not the reality most of us live in, with the joys existing under a constant barrage of bills, global conflict, illness, want. It’s one where the universe is conspiring to keep you alive in every way, down to the tiniest particles of air.
