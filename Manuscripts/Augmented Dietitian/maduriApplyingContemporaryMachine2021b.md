Methods: "Using real-world data from the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics Health Informatics Infrastructure, we use state-of-the-art clustering techniques to identify 2 phenotypes characterizing the episodes of nutrition care observed in the National Quality Improvement (NQI) registry data set.To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has applied machine learning to nutrition care RWD documented by registered dietitian nutritionists (RDNs).The following major steps were performed in this study: data preprocessing, feature engineering, feature selection, episode-of-care clustering, and problem-resolution classification."
Findings: "The 2 phenotypes identified from recorded Nutrition Care Process data in the NQI exhibit a strong correspondence with the clinical expertise of registered dietitian nutritionists. For one of these phenotypes, it was possible to implement state-of-the-art classification techniques to predict the nutrition problem-resolution status of an episode of care. Prediction results show that the assessment of nutrition history, number of recorded visits in the episode, and use of nutrition counseling interventions were significantly and positively correlated with problem resolution. Meanwhile, evaluations of nutrition history that were not within the desired ranges were significantly and negatively correlated with problem resolution. Finally, we assess the usefulness of the current NQI data set and data model for supporting the application of contemporary machine learning methods to the data set. We also suggest ways of enhancing the NQI since registered dietitian nutritionists are encouraged to continue to contribute patient cases in this and other registry nutrition studies.Together, the NCP model and terminology, the ANDHII data collection platform and the NQI data set have opened up new opportunities for generating RWE from nutrition care practice and understanding progress in related health outcomes. For the first time, we have assessed the feasibility of applying machine learning approaches to data extracted from the NQI data set to predict and explain problem resolution, per episode of care. Distinct phenotypes for episodes of care were identified and the observed problem-resolution rates were reported for each phenotype.  Various methods were implemented and compared for predicting the resolution status of an episode.The 2 phenotypes for episodes of care that were identified provide a data-driven confirmation of widely held professional clinical judgments of RDNs. Namely, nutrition problems that were clinical in nature appeared resistant to nutrition-based treatments (as captured in phenotype P2), and intake problems due to knowledge deficits tended to be more amenable to treatment (as captured in phenotype P1).Prediction models for problem resolution all performed reasonably well on episodes belonging to phenotype P1, which suggests that the data being recorded in the NQI are informative ofproblem outcomes. Eleven statistically significant features were identified for predicting episode problem-resolution status within phenotype P1. Among them, use of nutrition counseling strategies is significantly and positively associated with problem resolution."
Conclusions: "Such evidence might be used to encourage RDNs to intensify efforts to identify and address intake problems.  Meanwhile, clinical nutrition problems were challenging to resolve and suggests that such problems may require more attention and research to increase our understanding of the underlying disease process. Also, it cannot be ruled out that clinical nutrition problems have components that require the active contribution of other disciplines within the healthcare team.Phenotypic interpretation of the data may lead to a novel delivery model for integrated care and decision, and eventually, to better RDN decision support for the profession. By contributing to ANDHII’s NQI, RDNs help with the needed evidence to influence policy decisions and reimbursement efforts. The presented study is the first of its type for NCP data. Moreover, since the NCPT is widely adopted by various electronic health records, the modifications to the taxonomy that the present analyses have inspired will improve the quality of RWE for nutrition care wherever such electronic health records are in use.In the future, with even bigger (and longer) data at hand, other phenotypes can potentially be discovered for different specialty areas and outcomes, which could be used to generate new hypotheses regarding how the care process could be personalized for different phenotypeThe authors believe that it is important, if not essential, for the nutrition anddietetic community to similarly assess the potential for RWE to improve the delivery of effective nutrition care.  Although the initial data set contained 38,475 data entries, almost a third of these were removed for reasons related to ambiguous or inconsistent documentation. By highlighting how RWE in the NQI can support big data methods, our findings may help motivate RDNs to engage in entering data in ANDHII’s NQI, with as much follow-up data as possible. Thus, RDNs have a leading role to play in frontier research based on observational data from practice.  In the future, with confirmation of the presented phenotypes and discovery of others, RDNs will be able to identify in advance those interventions most likely to succeed in achieving problem resolution and improve further health outcomes in a time and cost-effective manner."
Limitations: "limitations. The NQI data set was limited in its ability to accurately represent all attributes of an episode of care. Since the use of the NQI is voluntary, RDNs are not required to record patient data in the registry on every visit. Therefore, the resolution status of an episode may be either an interim or final status of an episode.  Also, it was not possible to determine the true number of visits in an episode, which could be greater than the number of recorded visits. According to the current data model, a patient who has lost 9 lg and a patient who has gained 9 lb would have the equivalent resolution status (ie, continued) in the NQI. Therefore, it may be meaningful to include additional levels of problem-resolution status, such as, continued—improving and continued—not improving. These status levels are expected to reflect the RDN’s expert judgment of trends related to the problem and can complement the objective data entries in the NQI. They could also be a useful summary for RDNs when patient care transitions between RDNs."
