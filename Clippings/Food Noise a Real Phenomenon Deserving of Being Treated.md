“Food noise” is experiencing a huge research moment. Patients have been talking about the phenomenon for the past [few years](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-gravity-of-weight/202601/no-longer-voice-a-closer-look-at-food-noise), especially on social media. The realization that GLP-1 drugs seemed to [dampen the impact](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/06/21/well/eat/ozempic-food-noise.html?unlocked_article_code=1.tlA.3Dba.Yfn4ZOTJT8aV&smid=url-share) of this unwanted preoccupation with food, or even make it disappear entirely, has caught researchers’ interest and has spurred a plethora of new studies, three definitions and related clinical tools, as well as hypotheses about the mechanisms underlying the phenomenon.

## ‘Relentless, Real’

“Even when I’m eating, I’m planning the next time I’m gonna’ eat,” one patient told Travis D. Masterson, PhD, director, Health, Ingestive Behavior, and Technology Laboratory at Penn State, University Park, Pennsylvania. “There’s a little voice in my head always reminding me about food and thinking about food,” said another. Although the specific content of this “food noise” varies from person to person, “what’s consistent is that it never stops,” Masterson told *Medscape Medical News*.

But when the GLP-1 market started to skyrocket, many patients on the drugs found that suddenly, the food noise *did* stop. “That abrupt shift made them realize how relentless it had been,” he said. “If you’re gradually brought into a state of agitation, you don’t notice the feelings as much. But when you have a sudden drop or a sudden shift, then it becomes very apparent.”

[Bariatric surgery](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/197081-overview) patients reported a similar but “less dramatic” shift, likely because surgery involves a longer process of behavior change and didn’t reach as many people, he added.

Key Points

- Food noise = persistent intrusive food thoughts; linked to maladaptive eating, distress.
- No universal definition; 3 proposed tools: FNQ, RAID-FN, food cue reactivity-based.
- FNQ/RAID-FN validated in adults with obesity; higher scores correlate with overeating.
- GLP-1 initiation + behavioral tx ↓ food noise more than behavioral tx alone at 1 month.
- Likely mechanisms: semi-starvation/diet cycling, neurologic dysregulation; GLP-1s may quiet brain cue reactivity.

“Some clinicians are dismissive of those feelings,” said David B. Allison, PhD, chief of nutrition and director, Children’s Nutrition Research Center, Baylor College of Medicine, Houston. “They’re focusing only on the physical aspects. They think \[overweight/ [obesity](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/123702-overview)\] is only about disease, [insulin](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/2089224-overview) levels, glucose levels, and heart attacks. They say they’re not in the business of lifestyle optimization.”

SUGGESTED FOR YOU

But that’s a “false dichotomy,” Allison told *Medscape Medical News*. “The fact that obesity has been classified as a disease and that people deserve treatment in no way undermines the idea that some people want to optimize their lives, including getting rid of food noise, or that some people have experiences we might typically associate with struggling with weight even if they’re not [obese](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/123702-overview).”

“We don’t say to a person with intense anxiety, ‘Who cares how you feel? There’s no physical manifestation, too bad,’” he said. “We have anti-anxiety treatments, and I think we have to have similar things for food noise.”

On a personal note, Allison said that he’s lean but works hard to stay that way, and experiences food noise himself. He asked, “Why aren’t I entitled to peace?”

## Just What Is Food Noise?

Researchers now are trying to zero in on a definition of food noise so it can be quantified and treated. At this point, there is no universally agreed-upon definition, but the three that have been proposed very recently share a common core.

Masterson’s lab was among the first to offer [a definition](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu15224809) of food noise as “heightened and persistent manifestations of food cue reactivity, often leading to intrusive thoughts and maladaptive eating behaviors.”

[Food cue reactivity](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11154-022-09724-x) is a term already used in the scientific literature, Masterson said. When patients first started describing food noise, “it matched up very well with that concept. And if food noise is connected to food cue reactivity in some way, we already have a good scientific basis to work from. We don’t necessarily have to reinvent the wheel in trying to understand food noise.”

A second definition emerged last year, when [Hanim E. Diktas, Louisiana University System, Baton Rouge, Louisiana, and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.24216) defined food noise as “persistent, intrusive thoughts about food that are disruptive to daily life and make healthy behaviors difficult.” The team produced a “Food Noise Questionnaire” (FNQ) to measure the phenomenon. The FNQ is a five-item scale validated in adults with obesity; higher scores, indicating more frequent and intrusive food thoughts, were associated with a greater tendency to overeat.

A subsequent [observational study](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1pumAZWLBc_m3hiXUXIuzTRpsp-KSlCmE/view) using the FNQ, presented at the European Congress on Obesity 2026, found that patients who initiated a GLP-1 alongside behavioral treatment showed significantly greater reductions in food noise scores after 1 month than those receiving behavioral treatment alone (adjusted mean change of -4.1 vs -1.2 points).

A third definition of food noise comes from an [expert panel](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41387-025-00382-x) led by Allison and Emily J. Dhurandhar, PhD, then chief scientific officer of Obthera, Inc., now at Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center in Lubbock, Texas: “persistent thoughts about food that are perceived by the individual as being unwanted and/or dysphoric and may cause harm to the individual, including social, mental, or physical problems.”

“Food noise is not always about a specific food,” Dhurandhar said. “It’s more incessant, more like a constant [rumination](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/916297-overview); it just keeps going, and it can also happen even if there’s no external cue,” she said. “Imagine someone lost in the woods, constantly thinking about where their next meal will come from. There’s no McDonald’s, and no advertisements. But their food noise likely would be very high.”

This team developed the [RAID-FN Inventory](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2025.108339) (Ro Allison Indiana Dhurandhar Food Noise Inventory), available in short (seven-item) and long (23-item) forms, which captures three dimensions — preoccupation with food, persistence of thoughts, and dysphoria arising from those thoughts.

“By measuring food noise, we can validate that the patient’s experience is real and we can take a patient’s food noise levels into account when we’re evaluating treatment outcomes,” Dhurandhar said. The RAID-FN’s dysphoria factor includes items like “I hide that I think about food so much because I don’t want others to think less of me.” This means that patients may feel shame about food noise and not bring it up at doctor visits, she suggested.

## What’s the Mechanism?

Beyond definitions and measuring tools, the mechanism(s) behind food noise also are under investigation. Dhurandhar pointed to the [Minnesota Starvation Experiments](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2013/10/hunger), when volunteers starved themselves so researchers could explore how to help people recover from starvation. Accounts from that research, she said, bear “an uncanny similarity” to how people describe food noise today, leading to a hypothesis that food noise may be triggered by semi-starvation.

“Weight loss is by definition a semi-starvation state, and repeated cycles of dieting may compound it,” she said. Both the FNQ and RAID show associations between food noise and frequency of dieting for weight loss, which supports that hypothesis.

Some clinicians dismiss food noise because of a “category error,” Allison noted. The assumption is that because food noise sounds like a psychological or behavioral issue, it isn’t also biological. But that’s not the case, in his view.

Allison proposed a neurologic basis for food noise that is still under investigation. His team is examining the extent to which food noise is at least partially driven by an overall breakdown in the neurologic system’s ability to regulate itself downward. He pointed to analogies in aging, citing the brain’s increasing difficulty filtering out noise in crowded rooms, the breakdown of neural inhibition that causes leg cramps and restless sleep, and the early olfactory loss that precedes a Parkinson’s disease diagnosis.

Getting obsessed about food, like having earworms with music, may be a function of getting older, he suggested, and food noise may be part of that same pattern.

Masterson suggested that food cue reactivity and craving happen in the brain, and that GLP-1s bind to receptors there, functioning as a signal to slow or stop eating. “If you take a GLP-1,” he said, “you’re essentially telling your body you’re eating when you’re not, and that helps explain why \[the drugs\] seem to quiet food noise so dramatically.”

That hypothesis also explains why food noise returns when patients stop the drugs. “When the drug clears your system, you’re back to the state you were in,” he said. “You’re no longer getting the added benefit of that hormone.”

## How Clinicians Can Help?

Masterson recently co-authored [a publication](https://scholarsphere.psu.edu/resources/4806181d-0f5e-4864-982b-eb07ea81d568/downloads/41537?download_token=eyJfcmFpbHMiOnsibWVzc2FnZSI6IkJBaHBBa0dpIiwiZXhwIjoiMjAyNi0wNi0yMlQxMToyOToxMC43MTFaIiwicHVyIjoiZG93bmxvYWRfcmVxdWVzdCJ9fQ%3D%3D--f0ed8c68215b42ebe39677f00eaad3f21a7de918) aimed at registered dietitians and diabetes specialists, but with implications for any clinician who is treating a patient with food noise. In it, he recommends the following strategies.

To help patients identify maladaptive food-related perceptions, ask questions about food-related thoughts; listen to the role food plays in the patient’s life; and assess whether the patient is describing physiological hunger or intrusive thoughts about food.

Counsel patients on the difference between physiological hunger and food noise; listen to patients’ experiences; distinguish between thinking about food when hungry or planning a meal, vs persistent food preoccupation.

To help patients identify triggers for food noise and build resilience to them, assess the strength of those triggers for the individual, including exposure to food advertisements in physical and virtual spaces, chronic stress, and lack of sleep; strategize ways to restructure physical environments and limit exposure to food cues; suggest keeping a regular meal schedule; and recommend listening to internal hunger and satiety cues.

Use an interdisciplinary approach by referring to other healthcare professionals as warranted; pairing an obesity medicine specialist, a registered dietitian, and behavioral health, gives patients the best range of tools.

“Food noise is a problem that is a bit more complex than many clinicians may be prepared for,” Masterson said. “Instead of telling patients to exercise or eat a certain way, we suggest referring to an obesity medicine specialist who has the clinical training and the proper set of treatments to help.”

For patients who don’t have a [fatty liver](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/175472-overview) and aren’t obese or diabetic but still have food noise, Allison suggested starting a GLP-1 at a modest dose and monitoring as appropriate. “As long as the patient is a sane adult who is fully informed of the facts and wants to take a GLP-1 agonist to diminish food noise, I think it’s totally appropriate.”

He also noted that incorporating validated food noise measures into clinical drug trials, something that has only recently begun, may eventually let clinicians match patients to medications based on specific symptoms, and give clinicians effective tools to work with.
