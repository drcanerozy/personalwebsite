## Ultra-Processed Food, Retold by HC Andersen

## A Fairy Tale of Food Science, Stripped Bare

[![Dr. David Ludwig](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fill:88:88/0*SuFgySBUWadOYKau.jpg)](https://medium.com/@davidludwigmd "Physician, Nutrition Researcher, and Public Health Advocate. #1 NY Times bestselling author ofALWAYS HUNGRY? and ALWAYS DELICIOUS")

a11y-light · September 3, 2025 (Updated: September 4, 2025) · Free: Yes

Should national nutrition policy target "ultra-processed foods" to prevent obesity and other chronic diet-related diseases, as many nutrition experts advocate?

In a new Perspective in *[New England Journal of Medicine](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMp2508694)*, I take a critical look at the science, which is surprisingly thin for such a celebrated notion.

I'm reminded of " [The Emperor's New Clothes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emperor%27s_New_Clothes) " by Hans Christian Andersen. We seem to be rolling out a red carpet, even though the evidence is threadbare!

#### What is Ultra-Processed Food (UPF)?

The notion of UPF was popularized by a Brazilian nutrition researcher in 2009 as part of the Nova food classification system. In its present form, [Nova has 4 groups](https://educhange.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/NOVA-Food-Classification-EduChange.pdf):

Group 1 — Foods that are unprocessed and minimally processed (such as by removal of inedible parts, drying, freezing, or pasteurization). These include whole kernel or coarsely milled grains, whole fruits, vegetables, beans, nuts, milk, eggs, fish, and meat.

Group 2 — Processed traditional culinary ingredients like oils and fats, sugar, and salt.

Group 3 — Processed food made by combining whole foods in Group 1 with ingredients in Group 2. For example, home-baked bread and cookies, pickled vegetables, fruit preserved in sugar, canned fish, and "artisanal" processed meats (that is, hand-crafted with traditional methods).

**Group 4 — UPF**, defined as"industrial formulations made entirely or mostly from substances extracted from foods (oils, fats, sugar, starch, and proteins), derived from food constituents (hydrogenated fats and modified starch), or synthesized in laboratories from food substrates or other organic sources (flavor enhancers, colors, and several food additives used to make the product hyperpalatable)."

*Critically, the presence of j* *[ust one main component or additive](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/public-health-nutrition/article/ultraprocessed-foods-what-they-are-and-how-to-identify-them/E6D744D714B1FF09D5BCA3E74D53A185)* *not typically used in traditional cooking makes a commercial product UPF*.

Clearly, a broad consensus exists that our food system is broken, contributing to the epidemic of chronic disease. To many, UPF looks like the prime suspect. A *Who's Who* of nutrition and public-health experts have called for adoption of Nova/UPF into national and international nutrition policy. Influential NGOs, including the [Food and Agriculture Organization](https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/5277b379-0acb-4d97-a6a3-602774104629/content) of the United Nations, back the idea. And countries around the world are rolling out laws and regulations.

But what is the basis for thinking that a focus on UPF will make Americans (and the rest of the world) any healthier?

#### Epidemiological Studies

Dozens of observational studies have examined the relationship between UPF and various health outcomes. Overall, UPF is consistently linked to risk for obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, dementia, behavioral disorders, and more. People eating a lot of UPF develop more health problems over time than people eating little UPF.

But these studies have 2 major problems. First, people who eat a lot of UPF are different from those who eat little in many ways, making direct comparisons difficult. This is technically called *confounding* — the bias that creeps in when other factors, not the one being studied, explain the apparent relationships.

To see this, we need only look at the customary Table 1 in these studies — baseline data showing how people differ in lifestyle and demographic characteristics at the start of the study. As you can see in this typical example, people eating the most UPF tend to have less education, have higher body weight, engage in less physical activity, smoke more, and consume more alcohol. So, is it the UPF or the associated risk factors that lead to higher rates of chronic disease? The investigators can try to sort this out in statistical models, but invariably, some confounding will remain.

![Orlich et al. AJCN 2022;115:1589–1601](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*dDbr_RaZGt79XLWx5j-Jbg.png)

The second issue is that **the associations go in all directions for individual food items within the UPF group**. In this study of cardiovascular disease:

- Positive for sugary beverages (consumption related to *more* disease)
- Negative for savory snacks (consumption related to *less* disease)
- Neutral for many other foods (consumption unrelated to disease)

This variability raises concern for imprecision.

#### Imprecision

Imprecision may sound like a technical scientific issue, but it goes to the heart of any public health intervention. Let's consider an analogy. You and a friend head out to the woods with an expensive set of arrows for target practice. But your friend has imprecise aim. A few of his arrows may hit somewhere in the target but others miss entirely and get lost in the trees. One veers off widely, hitting you in the leg, although you were standing a supposedly safe distance away.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*2Qh2NXGRc3AfxeviM9h7sA.png)

These are the 3 problems with imprecise public health policy. It may:

- Fail to fully achieve the goal (hit the bull's eye)
- Waste time and money, and
- Cause harm

Let's do a thought experiment. Suppose researchers hypothesized that the color of the food in people's diets has a major effect on chronic disease. To test this idea, they run observational studies and find that, sure enough, white bread, white rice, and table sugar are strongly associated with worse outcomes. Despite lots of variation among specific foods, overall, people who eat more white foods are less healthy than those who eat less.

Based on this finding, should we focus on food color in national dietary guidelines?

Of course not. Some foods we consider healthy would be stigmatized, like yogurt, tofu, and cauliflower, whereas some foods we consider unhealthy would slip through, such as a cola and brownies. Furthermore, the food industry could easily game the system: they could add caramel coloring to white bread. These same problems apply to Nova/UPF.

#### Mechanisms — Major Nutrients

To avoid these pitfalls, we need to drill down on mechanisms, to get a better handle on **cause and effect**. Related to one of the two fundamental criteria for UPF:

*Mechanical processing of carbohydrate is harmful*

- Wheat berries ➡️ white bread
- [Oat groats (or steel-cut oats) ➡️ instant oatmeal](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/103/3/e26/62189/High-Glycemic-Index-Foods-Overeating-and-Obesity)
- Apple ➡️ juice ➡️ sugar

*But mechanical processing of proteins and fats is typically harmless or beneficial*

- Olive ➡️ olive oil
- Peanuts ➡️ peanut butter
- Sesame seeds ➡️ tahini
- Cacao ➡️ dark chocolate
- Soy beans ➡️ tofu
- Steak ➡️ hamburger

This is because processing accelerates the [digestion rate of carbohydrates](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/194907), increasing blood glucose and insulin levels after eating. High insulin drives calories from the meal into storage in fat tissue, leaving fewer calories for the rest of the body — [so we get hungrier sooner](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916522005172?via%3Dihub). In contrast, proteins and fats digest slowly, even when processed, and they raise insulin less.

(Fats can be chemically harmed, such as by partial hydrogenation, but the resulting *trans* -fats have been largely removed from the food supply.)

To approach this phenomenon from another angle, think of common binge foods with just 1 main nutrient:

*Only carbohydrates — easy*

- Sugary drinks
- Most packaged breakfast cereals
- Baked chips
- Low-fat muffin
- Pretzels
- Dry popcorn
- Candy
- Fat-free cookies (for example, [Snackwell's](https://www.reddit.com/r/nostalgia/comments/lkt6wb/did_anyone_elses_mom_buy_snackwell_cookies_in_the/))

*Only protein or fat — not so easy!*

- Burger no bun?
- Turkey breast?
- Egg whites?
- Stick of butter?
- Olive oil?
- Unsweetened cacao?

#### Mechanisms — Additives

This same concern applies to chemical additives, the second criterion for designating a food as UPF. Some may be harmful: emulsifiers can disrupt the gut lining and nitrites can cause gastrointestinal cancer. However, others may be harmless or beneficial, such as carbonation, lactic acid, amino acids, stevia, and various dietary fibers.

#### Misleading and Burdening the Public

Because Nova/UPF lumps all forms of industrial processing and additives into one basket, regardless of mechanisms, it results in many non-sensical and burdensome recommendations. To avoid UPF (Group 4), you can have:

- Cornflakes with sugar added at home BUT NOT a high-protein, high-fiber, low-sugar packaged cereal
- A packaged snack food with added sugar and salt BUT NOT added stevia
- White bread purchased from an artisanal shop BUT NOT most store-bought varieties
- Tofu prepared laboriously at home from soy beans BUT NOT plant-based meat substitutes or high-protein nutrition bars
- Ribeye BUT NOT a commercially prepared burger at 1/3 the cost

*This is no way to make public policy!*

Though not often discussed, Nova/UPF has at least inadvertently elitist leanings. Would people be healthier if they cook from scratch and purchase only artisanal foods? Sure, but many don't have the time or money for that.

It's like Marie Antoinette saying, "Let them eat steak!"

#### Clinical Trials: "Worse Than Worthless — They're Misleading"

That's how Harvard's Walter Willett, the world's most highly cited nutrition researcher, [described trials](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/01/13/why-is-the-american-diet-so-deadly) like the one relied upon by experts to justify immediate public health intervention.

[In that trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/), done at the National Institutes of Health and published in 2019, 20 adults were housed on a metabolic ward and fed either "unprocessed" or ultra-processed diets for 2 weeks each. **The key finding:** participants ate about 500 calories a day more on the ultra-processed diet.

The trial had all the bells and whistles that can impress public health authorities — diets were carefully prepared by study staff, participants were kept under 24/7 observation, and data were meticulously collected. So, why do Willett and I ([among others](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131\(19\)30307-9)) hold the study in disdain?

*First, consider the meals used to distinguish the 2 diets.* Is it earth-shattering that people might like, and for a short while eat more:

- Eggs, turkey bacon, & cheese on an English muffin with tater tots VERSUS oatmeal, blueberries, & raw almonds (Breakfast, Study Day 3)?
- Cheeseburger & fries VERSUS salmon & green beans (Lunch, Day 6)?
- A steak & cheese burrito VERSUS an "entrée salad with green leaf lettuce, tomatoes, cucumbers, carrots, black beans (cooked from dried), corn (cooked from frozen), & avocado" (Dinner, Day 4)???

**But does this prove it's the ultra-processing?**

(Photos of all study meals in the paper supplement [S1, Table S1](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131\(19\)30248-7#mmc1))

On a technical but critical point, the ultra-processed diet had almost twice the energy density (the calories per bite, excluding beverages) than the unprocessed diet. This difference in energy density is more than enough to account for the 500 calorie per day difference in food intake entirely, as has [been known for decades](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9497184/). (Energy density influences eating behavior for a few days or weeks, but has little effect on body weight [over the long term](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18444133/).)

Another thought experiment. Suppose you studied research participants under 2 experimental conditions for 2 weeks each:

Condition #1 — A high energy density diet (e.g., burger and fries) consumed in silence.

Condition #2 — A low energy density diet (e.g., salmon and green beans) listening to classical music.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*7-qbJxvq7rq9yye4n8zumw.png)

If people ate less in Condition #2, would you conclude that classical music has a novel effect on eating behavior to prevent obesity? Should we base obesity prevention on Bach?

Though participants in that condition might have felt more relaxed, the study provides no relevant evidence one way or the other. The effect could have been caused by the difference in energy density or possibly other uncontrolled factors.

Myriad factors affect how much a person eats on any particular day, among them [utensil size](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153875/), [plate color](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29679981/), [room temperature](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32693846/), and [social setting](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19501755/). But we don't eat with small spoons on colorless plates in a warm room with strangers in the hopes of losing much weight — we understand these effects are temporary.

**IN FACT, the key study finding — the difference in daily calorie consumption between the 2 diets — weakened rapidly, even during the short, 2-week time frame of the trial**. With extrapolation, the effect would extinguish after another 2 weeks.

In light of these deficiencies, I struggle to understand how the smartest, most discerning experts in the field could consider this:

- " [as good a clinical trial as you can get](https://www.theguardian.com/food/2020/feb/13/how-ultra-processed-food-took-over-your-shopping-basket-brazil-carlos-monteiro) "
- " [the most important study ever done to explain weight gain](https://www.foodpolitics.com/2024/06/dietary-guidelines-ii-where-is-rigorous-nutrition-research/) "
- " [landmark findings that the processing of the foods makes a huge difference in how much a person eats](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2019/05/16/723693839/its-not-just-salt-sugar-fat-study-finds-ultra-processed-foods-drive-weight-gain) "

Three other controlled trials on UPF have been published so far ([1-week](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39267249/), [3-weeks](https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131\(25\)00360-2), and [8-weeks](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40760353/)), all with similar design limitations. In the [largest and longest](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40760353/), the participants lost weight on both (!) minimally- and ultra-processed diets, although weight loss was reportedly about 2 pounds more on the minimally processed diet over 8 weeks. Among the 55 participants, 12 dropped out of the study — all from the minimally processed diet. (The likelihood of this happening by chance is less than 1 in 1000). Leaving aside the [serious bias that could arise](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMe030054) from so much missing data on one diet, **is it surprising that people would eat a bit less, and lose a bit more weight, from a diet many seemed not to like and were unwilling to follow?**

We would never approve a weight loss drug based on 2-week or 2-month data, even if — in contrast to the present situation — the trials were designed and executed meticulously. How can we justify a massive public health intervention this way?

#### Lack of Independent Review

Nova/UPF is the brainchild of just one research group, and it has undergone only minor revisions since first being proposed 16 years ago. No independent expert commission or governmental agency has conducted a comprehensive examination to determine if Nova is more robust than other classification systems, if addition of nutrient-based criteria would improve precision, and if any such system will work better than just advising people to avoid junk food.

And if that weren't enough to raise red flags, the Food and Drug Administration recently expressed concerns "about the full ability of UPF classification systems to accurately capture the characteristics of UPFs that may impact health" and [asked the public](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/07/25/2025-14089/ultra-processed-foods-request-for-information) for assistance defining it.

*Practically speaking, we don't even know what the term UPF means!*

#### We've Been Down This Road Before

In the 1960s and 70s, rates of obesity and cardiovascular disease were on the rise. The prime suspect at the time was dietary fat. Fat has twice the calories per gram as carbohydrate or protein. Epidemiological studies suggested that people and populations eating the most fat were heavier and had higher rates of chronic disease than those eating the least fat. Short-term clinical trials — with similar problems to those discussed above — seemed to confirm the benefits of a low-fat diet.

Some experts [expressed concern](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/92686/) for the weakness of the evidence and risks of unintended consequences. However, those voices of caution were disregarded. The leadership argued that any gaps in the evidence shouldn't delay public health action. Like UPF today, the case against dietary fat *seemed* so clear. If you don't want fat on your body or in your coronary arteries, don't put it in your mouth — thus, the low-fat diet craze of the late 20th century.

[We know how that turned out](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2564564). American's cut back on fat as a proportion of their diets and instead ate more processed carbohydrates in the widely held belief that you [can't get fat on sugars](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7598083/) and starches. Unfortunately, rates of obesity and diabetes skyrocketed.

#### Conclusions

I was [an early supporter](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/896031) of Nova/UPF as a conceptual framework to explore how processing might influence the health effects of food, beyond conventional nutrient considerations. However, the evidence for basing national chronic disease prevention strategy on UPF as defined by Nova — one among [many processing-based systems](https://foodandnutritionresearch.net/index.php/fnr/article/view/12217) — is exceptionally weak.

Nova/UPF is:

- Inconsistent in observational studies (associations with health move in opposite directions across specific UPF food categories)
- Underwhelming — to say the least — in clinical trials
- Imprecise and ill-defined for regulatory purposes

Thus, the rush to adopt Nova/UPF could cause more harm than good, by:

- Demonizing healthy food
- Endorsing unhealthy food
- Confusing regulators
- Burdening the public
- Inviting industry manipulation
![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*AF37icysWEmFhxM7IeQ-aw.png)

In this light, UPF looks less like solid science than a romantic ideal — harking back to a mythic era of farm-fresh, home-cooked meals with family by the hearth — more fantasy than foundation for nutrition policy.

Which doesn't mean we must postpone public health action to prevent chronic disease. Pending further research on Nova/UPF and other such classification systems, we should focus on the processed foods for which we have strong evidence of harms.

I'm reminded of the catchphrase coined by James Carville, a political consultant in the 1992 presidential campaign — [It's the economy, stupid!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%27s_the_economy,_stupid)

For chronic disease prevention, the same could be said about **[refined grains, sugar, and other highly processed carohydrates](https://www.bmj.com/content/361/bmj.k2340)**.
