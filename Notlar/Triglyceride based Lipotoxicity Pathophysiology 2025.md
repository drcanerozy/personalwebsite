- Triglycerides (TGs), a category of lipid formed by three fatty acid chains (usually of a long-chain variety) attached to a glycerol backbone, are the most abundant lipids in the human body. They can be stored in the white adipose tissue (WAT), serving as a long-term energy reservoir, or packaged with apolipoproteins to form lipoproteins for delivery to different tissues and organs through the bloodstream or lymphatic system. In many different cell types, TGs are stored in lipid droplets (LDs), and participate in a wide range of biological functions [1]. The overall abundance of TGs is precisely regulated between absorption, de novo lipogenesis, storage, and utilization under physiological conditions (s. )
- TG metabolism in physiological conditions There are three primary sources of TGs (or FFAs) in the human body: intestinal absorption of dietary fats, de novo lipogenesis in the liver, and lipolysis of WAT. Postprandially, intestine-originated TGs are transported as chylomicron (CM), whereas hepatic de novo synthesis of TGs from excess glucose is triggered by postprandial insulin signaling, with such TGs secreted into the bloodstream as very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL). These TG-rich lipoproteins gradually undergo lipoprotein lipase (LPL)-mediated hydrolysis on endothelial surfaces to release FFAs for peripheral tissue uptake.  CMs are gradually processed into CM remnants, and VLDL is transformed into intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL). The IDLs are either taken up directly by the liver or further metabolized to LDLs by hepatic lipase. LDLs are finally cleared by hepatic receptor-mediated endocytosis [1]. During fasting, decreased glycemia inhibits insulin secretion from pancreatic islets and stimulates glucagon secretion. In response to this hormonal change, WAT undergoes a shift toward the hydrolysis of stored TGs to release glycerol and FFAs into the bloodstream. These molecules are subsequently transported to most organs as fue (s. 2)
- TGs are theoretically not toxic per se; instead, the direct effector of lipotoxicity should be attributed to the productor of their lipolysis: FFAs (s. 3)

- Ceramides 
- Ceramides are integral components of membranes and play a crucial role in cell signaling. There are three major pathways for the generation of ceramides: (i) de novo, (ii) through sphingomyelin hydrolysis, and (iii) through the salvage pathway (breakdown of complex sphingolipids) [18]. In the de novo pathway, ceramides are generated from palmitate and serine; thus, oversupply of FFAs like palmitate may favor the de novo pathway of ceramides [16]. Overproduction of ceramides has been shown to increase mitochondrial outer membrane permeability and inhibit oxidative phosphorylation [19]. They can also lead to insulin resistance by inhibiting the activation of AKT [16]. Ceramides were found to enhance the translocation of CD36, a key fatty acid transporter, toward the cell surface [20], which in turn subsequently worsens the intracellular accumulation of lipid and stress responses (s. 4)
- İnflammation
- FFAs can trigger inflammatory pathways by direct activation of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling [24] or by inducing a PKCdependent inflammation response through increased diacylglycerol (DAG) levels [25]. FFAinduced oxidative stress can also subsequently activate the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) pathway; moreover, FFA-induced ER stress responses also intersect with inflammatory signals [26] (s. 5)

Ingested dietary fats are broken down into free fatty acids (FFAs) in the intestine. They are then reassembled into TGs and packaged into chylomicrons (CMs) to transport dietary TGs from enterocytes into the circulation. In the postprandial phase, hepatic de novo lipogenesis converts carbohydrates into TGs packaged into very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) to transport endogenous TGs into the circulation. In the capillaries, endothelial lipoprotein lipase (LPL) converts CMs and VLDL-TGs into FFAs and glycerol, which are taken up by adipocytes and muscle cells via the transporter CD36. The pancreatic endocrine cell hormones, insulin and glucagon, serve central roles in the regulation of lipid metabolism. Insulin promotes the synthesis and storage of lipids, whereas glucagon inhibits lipogenesis and promotes lipolysis. Abbreviation: LDLR, low density lipoprotein receptor. #figure

Ectopic TG deposition can be observed not only in classic visceral sites (e.g., the omentum and liver) but also in the exocrine pancreas, renal parenchyma, skeletal muscle, brain, bone marrow, thyroid, sex glands, and probably ubiquitously throughout the body. This TG deposition plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of a wide range of diseases, including chronic pancreatitis, chronic kidney disease, osteoporosis, hypothyroidism, neurodegenerative diseases, infertility, and immune dysfunction. The fundamental mechanisms of TG-based lipotoxicity are similar across different tissues and organs (e.g., involving oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction). However, there are also some tissue- and organ-specific mechanisms due to functional heterogeneity and variations in lipid metabolism between tissues and organs.
